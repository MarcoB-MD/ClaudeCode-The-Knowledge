# Thinking scientifically: why it's hard, why it matters, and a practical toolkit

**Author:** Peter Attia
**Type:** Podcast (The Drive, Episode #389)
**Published:** 2026-04-27

---

## Summary

About half the episode listened to so far. Attia opens by framing scientific thinking as a skill that "sits upstream of nearly every decision you make about health, policy, risk, and even how to evaluate other people in this space." It is not a laboratory skill but a general capacity for evaluating claims, updating beliefs when evidence changes, and figuring out who to trust when you cannot do the analysis yourself — which, as he notes, is most of the time for most people.

The episode covers four things: (1) what scientific thinking actually is, beyond time in a lab; (2) why it is so hard for us, which has less to do with intelligence than expected; (3) what an individual can do to get better at it; (4) how to find trustworthy people when you cannot do the analysis yourself.

One idea threads through the whole episode: **the goal of thinking scientifically is not simply to be right — it's to be less wrong over time.** Science is a process built around that principle.

**What scientific thinking is.** It is not something scientists do in labs while the rest of us receive results. It is a way of engaging with claims about the world — any claims, not just ones with a citation attached. At its core it means: generating hypotheses (possible explanations for why something might be the way it is), testing those hypotheses against evidence, updating beliefs when the evidence changes, and tolerating uncertainty throughout. It means separating what you want to be true from what the evidence suggests is true — and recognizing how often those two are in tension.

Feynman, whom Attia returns to several times: *"The first principle is not to fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool."* Scientific thinking means being more invested in the process that produced a conclusion than in the conclusion itself. Most people evaluate claims by asking "is it true?" A scientific thinker asks first: how did they arrive at this? What's the evidence? How strong is it? What are the alternative explanations?

**"I don't know."** Attia emphasizes this as critically underused. "I don't know" is always the first honest answer to any scientific question; "our best understanding based on available evidence" is the second. In practice, out of ease and confidence, we skip straight to the second answer and drop the uncertainty — and in doing so, we lose what makes scientific thinking scientific.

**Two core functions of science:** ruling things out, and getting less wrong over time.

**Why scientific thinking is hard.** We evolved over millions of years as social creatures, not as rational analytical thinkers. The empirical approach is only about 400 years old — a blink relative to our evolutionary history. Structural safeguards — peer review, pre-registration, blinding — exist specifically to compensate for the cognitive limitations our evolved minds cannot shed through willpower alone.

Attia then introduces a series of practical heuristics (four or five in total; two covered so far):

**Heuristic 1 — Certainty as an alarm signal.** Whenever we feel very certain, treat that certainty as a warning. Ask: is it coming from emotions, from trust in a person, or from genuinely compelling data? What alternatives might explain the same phenomenon?

**Heuristic 2 — Judge the process, not the conclusion.** Examine the method by which a conclusion was reached, not just whether the conclusion sounds right. How strong is the method? Does it actually warrant the conclusion?

## Key Ideas

- **Scientific thinking sits upstream of nearly every decision** — it is not confined to labs; it is the skill of evaluating any claim about the world, updating beliefs as evidence changes, and knowing who to trust when you cannot do the analysis yourself.
- **The goal is not to be right — it is to be less wrong over time** — this framing threads the entire episode; science is a self-correcting process, not a delivery system for finished truths.
- **Process over conclusion** — most people ask "is it true?"; a scientific thinker asks "how did they arrive at this?", "what's the evidence?", "how strong is it?", "what are the alternative explanations?"
- **Feynman: you are the easiest person to fool** — the first principle is not to fool yourself; the greatest risk of self-deception comes from within, not from external sources.
- **"I don't know" is the first honest answer** — in practice we skip straight to "our best understanding" and drop the uncertainty; in doing so we lose what makes scientific thinking scientific.
- **Science has two core functions: ruling things out, and getting less wrong over time** — not proving things true, but eliminating the false and narrowing the range of the plausible.
- **Scientific thinking is hard because we did not evolve for it** — we evolved as social creatures; the empirical approach is only ~400 years old; our cognitive architecture was not built for it.
- **Structural safeguards exist to compensate for evolved limitations** — peer review, pre-registration, and blinding are not bureaucratic formalities; they are designed to protect against biases we cannot shed through willpower alone.
- **Heuristic 1 — certainty is an alarm** — when you feel very certain, ask why: is it emotion, trust in the person, or genuinely compelling data? Strong certainty should prompt more scrutiny, not less.
- **Heuristic 2 — evaluate the method, not just the conclusion** — how strong is the process? Does it actually warrant the claim? A conclusion that sounds right reached by a weak method is not reliable.

## Key Words

**Scientific Thinking** — a way of engaging with claims about the world by generating hypotheses, testing them against evidence, updating beliefs when evidence changes, and tolerating uncertainty; not confined to laboratories but applicable to any claim; the skill that sits upstream of nearly every decision about health, policy, and risk. *"Thinking scientifically is a way of engaging with claims about the world, any claims, not just ones that come with a citation attached."*

**"Less Wrong Over Time"** — Attia's central framing of the goal of scientific thinking: not to achieve certainty or simply be right, but to continuously reduce error through a self-correcting process. *"The goal of thinking scientifically is not simply to be right. It's to be less wrong over time."*

**Hypothesis** — a possible explanation for why something might be the way it is, or how something works; the starting point of scientific inquiry before testing against evidence. *"Generating hypotheses, possible explanations for why something might be the way it is."*

**Ruling Out** — one of science's two core functions alongside getting less wrong over time: eliminating explanations that are inconsistent with the evidence, narrowing the space of plausible claims. *"One of the most useful ways to think about science, especially in medicine, is by focusing on two of its core functions. The first is ruling things out."*

**Tolerating Uncertainty** — the disposition required for scientific thinking: maintaining acknowledgment of what remains unknown throughout the process rather than prematurely collapsing it into false confidence. *"It means tolerating uncertainty throughout this entire process."*

**"I Don't Know"** — Attia's first honest answer to any scientific question; the acknowledgment of uncertainty that precedes "our best understanding based on available evidence"; frequently dropped in practice, and when it is, what makes scientific thinking scientific is lost. *"'I don't know' can always be the first answer to any scientific theory."*

**Updating Beliefs** — revising one's positions when evidence changes; a core component of scientific thinking that distinguishes a scientific thinker from someone who holds fixed conclusions regardless of new information. *"It means updating your beliefs when the evidence changes."*

**Process vs. Conclusion** — the reformulation of what scientific thinkers focus on: rather than asking "is it true?", they ask "how did they arrive at this?", "what's the evidence?", "how strong is it?", "what are the alternative explanations?" *"Scientific thinking means being more invested in the process that produced a conclusion than in the conclusion itself."*
