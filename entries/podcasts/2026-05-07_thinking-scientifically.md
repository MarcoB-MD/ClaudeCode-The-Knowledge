# Thinking scientifically: why it's hard, why it matters, and a practical toolkit

**Author:** Peter Attia
**Type:** Podcast (The Drive, Episode #389)
**Published:** 2026-04-27

---

## Summary

Attia frames the entire episode around one idea: **the goal of thinking scientifically is not simply to be right — it's to be less wrong over time.** The episode covers four things: what scientific thinking is, why it is hard for us as a species, five individual heuristics for thinking more scientifically, and how to carefully outsource judgment to trusted experts.

**Part 1 — What scientific thinking is.** Not a lab skill — a way of engaging with any claim about the world. At its core: generating hypotheses, testing them against evidence, updating beliefs when evidence changes, and tolerating uncertainty throughout. The key reframe: be more invested in the process that produced a conclusion than in the conclusion itself. Most people ask "is it true?"; a scientific thinker asks "how did they arrive at this?", "what's the evidence?", "how strong?", "what are the alternatives?" "I don't know" and "it depends" are often the most honest available answers — in practice we skip straight to "our best current understanding" and drop the uncertainty, losing what makes scientific thinking scientific.

Two core functions of science: **ruling things out**, and **getting less wrong over time.** Hard proof exists only in formal logic and mathematics. The rest of science builds confidence in explanations that survive contact with data. George Box: "All models are wrong, but some are useful." Example: Newton's theory of gravity was not a proof but has survived and been refined by Einstein — GPS satellites must adjust their clocks by 38 microseconds per day to correct for time dilation. When evidence is overwhelming (smoking causes cancer — enormous hazard ratios, mechanistic understanding, dose-response relationships), the gap between our least-wrong model and capital-T true becomes vanishingly small. But most questions in medicine live in the middle: evidence suggestive but imperfect, models useful but incomplete. Dietary cholesterol was treated as settled for decades; the relationship was far more complex than the simple causal chain. Some of what we currently treat as settled simply is not. We must make decisions based on the best available knowledge while staying aware it is rarely the whole truth — "holding room for doubt and living confidently anyway." Scientific thinking is a practice, not an achievement. It can atrophy.

**Part 2 — Why scientific thinking is hard.** Not a matter of intelligence — it is biologically unnatural. We are primates who evolved as social animals for ~50 million years; survival depended on group standing; exile was often death. Our brains were optimized for social navigation: reading faces, building alliances, signaling loyalty. The empirical scientific method is only ~400 years old, built on top of 50 million years of primate social cognition. Social information can and will override logical information — not only in uneducated people but as a basic feature of human cognition.

Science requires almost the opposite of what we evolved for: holding multiple hypotheses simultaneously, tolerating uncertainty for years, understanding conditional probability and effect size, willingness to change deeply-held and socially costly beliefs.

Despite this, we invented corrective tools — Attia calls them **"prosthetics for objectivity":** peer review, blind experiments, pre-registration, statistical frameworks. A double-blind clinical trial is an explicit admission that even well-trained experts cannot evaluate outcomes without being biased by what they hope to find, so we remove that information. Science institutionalized productive disagreement: "We don't trust any one of us, but we trust the process."

**Part 3 — Five individual heuristics.**

**1 — Treat certainty as a cue to slow down.** Certainty is a feeling, not an indicator of truth. The brain generates it for social reasons — consensus in your feed, emotional resonance, familiarity, repetition, speaker confidence — none of which have anything to do with whether the claim is correct. When you notice certainty, ask: why do I believe this? If the answer is social (everyone agrees, the person sounded confident, I want it to be true) — that's a red flag. If you've seen and understood the data, considered alternatives, and still find the conclusion most compelling — you're in a better place. The questioning deepens over time: "question your certainty, question your questioning."

**2 — Judge the process, not just the conclusion.** Ask how they arrived at the claim, not whether the conclusion sounds right. A good process can produce a wrong conclusion; a bad process that produces a right conclusion got there by accident and won't be reliable going forward. Detox cleanse example: starts from a real observation (you don't feel well, environmental toxins exist) and jumps straight to a marketed solution with none of the work in between — no described mechanism, no blinding, no control group. Even a real effect (headaches clear) tells you nothing about the cause; cutting processed food, not "toxin removal," may be doing the work. Supplement "third-party testing" example: often only tests for heavy metal contamination, not whether the capsule contains what it claims.

**3 — Notice when identity is doing your thinking.** Coalition thinking is hardwired. If you believe your team has the right answer on every issue, that is not evidence you found the right team — it is evidence that group identity is doing your thinking for you. Galileo (famous but abstract). More instructive: Semmelweis and handwashing. In the 1840s at Vienna General Hospital, the doctor-staffed clinic had 5x the childbed fever mortality of the midwife clinic. Semmelweis traced the cause: doctors were carrying material from autopsies directly to deliveries; midwives did not perform autopsies. Requiring chlorinated lime handwashing dropped mortality from 18% to under 2%, some months to zero. The medical establishment rejected it — partly because germ theory didn't exist yet (miasma theory was dominant, so "invisible material on your hands causes disease" didn't fit the framework). But layered underneath the stated scientific objection was an identity-level threat: accepting Semmelweis meant accepting that doctors' own hands had been vectors of death. The stated scientific objection gave cover to the unstated identity defense. This pattern — identity-based motivation hiding behind scientific-sounding skepticism — undoubtedly happens today.

**4 — Don't confuse criticism with understanding.** It is vastly easier to criticize a study than to design and run one. Every study ever published can be criticized: sample size could be bigger, follow-up longer, control group not perfectly matched, residual confounding, surrogate endpoint, generalizability. The question is not "can this study be criticized?" (always yes) but "is this study informative despite its limitations?" Answering that requires synthesis and judgment. Brandolini's Law (bullshit asymmetry principle): the energy to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude larger than what's needed to produce it. Someone committed to casting doubt can always outrun someone committed to building understanding. Be wary of people who only criticize and never synthesize.

**5 — Outsource your thinking carefully.** No human can navigate the modern world without relying on expertise. The goal: build a **personal board of advisors** — two or three trusted people or outlets per domain. Attia evaluates potential advisors in three layers:

*Layer 1 — Who is this person?* Credentials set a Bayesian prior — not conclusive, but a meaningful starting point. Do they show their work? Engage with criticism? Acknowledge what they don't know? Track record matters; no one is an expert in everything. Kary Mullis (inventor of PCR, Nobel Prize in Chemistry) denied HIV causes AIDS; his ideas drove South African policy in the early 2000s and likely caused 250,000+ deaths. Are they explaining or performing? Jargon used to impress rather than inform is a red flag.

*Layer 2 — How are they thinking?* Do they show their reasoning, not just conclusions? Steel-manning vs. straw-manning: a steel-manner presents the strongest version of the opposing argument and explains why they still disagree; a straw-manner knocks down the weakest version. Are opinions anchored to data? Feynman lecture (~1960s): "If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. That's all there is to it." Do they acknowledge uncertainty, and have they publicly changed their mind? Publicly updating is one of the strongest trust signals available — the social cost is enormous; doing it anyway tells you this person cares more about getting it right than appearing infallible.

*Layer 3 — Red flags.* How do they make money? A pitch that always ends in a promo code or affiliate link is an advertisement. More insidious: engagement-based incentives (always contrarian, "everyone is lying except me") — their product is your attention, not your wellbeing. Is their position consistent with the weight of evidence? Scientific consensus is not a vote; it forms when evidence becomes so overwhelming that virtually all qualified people who engage with it arrive at the same conclusion. Countering it is legitimate science — but requires data (new evidence, critique of interpretation, identification of missing pieces), not vibes or ideology. Are they always right and everyone else always wrong? The rhetorical move "they used to say eggs were bad so why trust science about anything" — science got the egg cholesterol story wrong and then updated it; that is the system working. Treating all scientific conclusions as equally shaky isn't skepticism — it's a performance of skepticism designed to let you ignore what you find inconvenient. "The existence of past errors isn't evidence that all current conclusions are wrong. It's evidence that the process works."

**Closing.** Three things to get better at: noticing when certainty and identity are misleading you; judging the quality of a process rather than just its conclusions; choosing who to trust when you can't do the analysis yourself.

## Key Words

**Scientific Thinking** — a way of engaging with claims about the world by generating hypotheses, testing them against evidence, updating beliefs when evidence changes, and tolerating uncertainty; not confined to laboratories but applicable to any claim; the skill that sits upstream of nearly every decision about health, policy, and risk. *"Thinking scientifically is a way of engaging with claims about the world, any claims, not just ones that come with a citation attached."*

**"Less Wrong Over Time"** — Attia's central framing of the goal of scientific thinking: not to achieve certainty or simply be right, but to continuously reduce error through a self-correcting process built around that principle. *"The goal of thinking scientifically is not simply to be right. It's to be less wrong over time."*

**Hypothesis** — a possible explanation for why something might be the way it is, or how something works; the starting point of scientific inquiry before testing against evidence. *"Generating hypotheses, possible explanations for why something might be the way it is."*

**Ruling Out** — one of science's two core functions: eliminating explanations that are inconsistent with the evidence, narrowing the space of plausible claims until only what cannot be eliminated remains. *"We rule things out one by one until we're left with the explanation we can't rule out."*

**Tolerating Uncertainty** — the disposition required for scientific thinking: maintaining acknowledgment of what remains unknown throughout the process, rather than prematurely collapsing it into false confidence. *"It means tolerating uncertainty throughout this entire process."*

**"I Don't Know"** — the first honest answer to any scientific question; frequently dropped in practice in favor of going straight to "our best understanding based on available evidence," and when dropped, what makes scientific thinking scientific is lost. *"'I don't know' can always be the first answer to any scientific theory."*

**Updating Beliefs** — revising one's positions when evidence changes; a core component of scientific thinking that distinguishes a scientific thinker from someone who holds fixed conclusions regardless of new information. *"It means updating your beliefs when the evidence changes."*

**Process vs. Conclusion** — the reformulation of what scientific thinkers focus on: rather than asking "is it true?", they ask "how did they arrive at this?", "what's the evidence?", "how strong?", "what are the alternative explanations?" *"Scientific thinking means being more invested in the process that produced a conclusion than in the conclusion itself."*

**Prosthetics for Objectivity** — Attia's term for peer review, blinding, pre-registration, and statistical frameworks; structural tools invented specifically to counteract our natural cognitive limitations because we've recognized we cannot trust our own unassisted judgment. *"I think of them as prosthetics for objectivity. They exist precisely because we've recognized… that we couldn't trust our own unassisted judgment."*

**Coalition Thinking** — the default human mode of evaluating claims through group membership and identity rather than evidence; hardwired into our cognition as a social species; the mechanism by which identity-based motivation can masquerade as scientific skepticism. *"Coalition thinking is our default mode… it can be the enemy of scientific thinking."*

**Miasma Theory** — the pre-germ-theory belief that disease was caused by "bad air" or noxious fumes; the dominant framework under which the medical establishment rejected Semmelweis's handwashing findings; illustrates how a legitimate scientific objection (within the prevailing theory) can provide cover for an identity-based defense.

**Steel-Manning** — presenting the strongest possible version of an opposing argument before disagreeing with it; contrasted with straw-manning (attacking the weakest version); a reliable signal of genuine intellectual engagement and willingness to update. *"A steel-man presents the strongest version of the opposing argument and then explains why they still disagree."*

**Brandolini's Law (Bullshit Asymmetry Principle)** — the energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude larger than what's needed to produce it; someone committed to casting doubt can always outrun someone committed to building understanding. *"The energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude larger than what's needed to produce it."*

**Scientific Consensus** — not a vote or popularity contest; forms when evidence becomes so overwhelming that virtually all qualified people who genuinely engage with it arrive at the same conclusion; can only be legitimately challenged with data. *"Consensus forms when the evidence in a particular area becomes so overwhelming that virtually all qualified people who genuinely engage with it arrive at the same conclusion."*

**Personal Board of Advisors** — Attia's practical heuristic for outsourcing judgment: for any domain that matters to you, identify two or three people or outlets whose judgment you trust and be honest about why; the goal is to cut through noise with the help of pre-vetted trusted sources.

**Bayesian Reasoning** — updating a prior probability in light of new evidence; credentials set a prior that should be updated based on how the person actually reasons; not treating credentials as conclusive or irrelevant, but as a starting point. *"Credentials set a prior, but that prior should be updated based on how the person actually reasons."*

**George Box's Maxim** — "All models are wrong, but some are useful"; a statement about the nature of scientific knowledge: we are not proving claims in some final absolute sense, but comparing explanations, testing predictions, and gradually gaining confidence in the ones that survive contact with data.
